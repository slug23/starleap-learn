The Transistor Switch

The big idea

The transistor lets a small current control a big one. Every automatic thing electricity does — sensing, deciding, computing — starts with the transistor family. Ours is the classic; your laptop packs billions of its cousins.

Part 1

The basics

One new part, one build, one big idea. This part is the lesson: finish it and you've done the day's work. Part 2, further down, is extra.

1 Today you will…

  • Meet the transistor: three legs, three names, and the one rule that keeps it alive.
  • Build a switch with no moving parts — and flip it with a wire that carries almost nothing.
  • Find out why a wire left dangling is no command at all.

2 Your kit today

  • Board with supply on the rails — cleared to bare rails last lesson — plus jumpers, a red LED, 220 Ω, 4.7 kΩ, multimeter
  • NPN transistor — the little black half-cylinder with a flat face and three legs (plus a spare)
  • For Part 2, if you get there: pot, capacitors, the two pushbuttons, the rest of your resistors — they stay in the box until you need them

3 Warm-up: stored light, on paper

Kits closed, one minute: draw lesson 6's store-and-spend circuit from memory — the capacitor, the path that fills it, the path that spends it through an LED, a button on each, and which leg gets the stripe. Blanks and guesses are fine; this is a check, not a test. Then open the answer and fix your drawing.

Check your drawing

One question for your neighbor before the boxes open: when the LED went dark, was the capacitor empty? (No — the meter still read well over a volt. Dark doesn't mean drained — only the reset drains it: a bare 220 Ω from node A to ground.) Keep your drawing — Part 2's timer is built from it.

4 New component: the transistor

Three legs, and each has a name and a job. The big current flows in at the collector and out at the emitter — but only when a small current is flowing into the base. Think of a faucet lever once: the effort at the handle is not the water. From here on, the real words: base current permits collector current — up to the transistor's gain times bigger. The collector's own circuit decides how much of that permission actually gets used.

The base is a diode

Inside, the base-to-emitter path behaves like a small LED: it starts conducting near ≈ 0.7 V and holds close to that as the current climbs. So the naked-LED rule applies here too: the base never connects straight to 5 V — it always gets its own resistor. A bare base across the supply is a huge current and a cooked transistor.

5 Build the switch

Symbol vs. part: match by name, not by shape

The drawing and the part will never look alike — schematics always point the collector toward + and the emitter toward ground, because the whole page reads downhill, while the package reads E-B-C in a flat row. Don't make the build imitate the picture. Find each wire's letter in the schematic and take it to that leg's row on the board.

  1. Flat face toward you, legs down: E · B · C into three neighboring rows. Say the legs out loud as you plug them.
  2. Collector loop: + rail → 220 Ω → LED (long leg toward +) → collector row. Emitter row → ground rail.
  3. Base: 4.7 kΩ from the base row to an empty row; a jumper from that row is your control wire — free end in your hand.
  4. Before power: trace the base with a finger. Its only way in is through the 4.7 kΩ — no wire goes straight from a rail to the base row.
  5. Power on. Predict all three, then test: control wire touched to the + rail? Touched to the ground rail? Dangling in the air?

On, off — and the dangling wire? Probably off too. But look closely at what "dangling" means: nothing is holding the base off. Touching + is a command; touching ground is a command; dangling is no command at all, and an uncommanded input is at the mercy of static, damp air, and luck. It behaved today; never design a circuit that needs it to. The rule, from here through the whole digital semester: an input you mean to be off gets wired to ground — told, not left to hope.

Still: you just operated a switch with no moving parts — flipped by a wire carrying a small fraction of the current it commands. Where lesson 2's slide switch needed a thumb, this one needs only a whisper of current — and anything that can make a whisper can now be a thumb.

Part 1 is done when…

  • Your LED lights when the control wire touches the + rail, and goes dark when it touches ground.
  • You can say why the dangling wire is not a third command.
  • You can point to the base, the collector, and the emitter — on your board and on the schematic.

All three? That's the lesson — nice work. Leave the switch standing: next lesson hires it exactly as it is. The words and the exit ticket are right below; Part 2 comes after them, if you want more.

6 Words to know

transistor
three legs; a small base current permits a big collector-to-emitter current
base · collector · emitter
the control leg · where the big current enters · where it leaves (the arrow)
NPN
our transistor's family — control current flows into the base
base resistor
the resistor every base gets, because the base behaves like a diode — never a bare base on the supply

8 Measure the switch itself

Black probe parked in the ground rail. Red probe on the transistor's own legs — the top of the leg, or a bare wire stub plugged into its row. Predict each before you measure:

MeasurementControl at +5 V (LED on)Control at ground (LED off)
Collector leg to ground — across the "switch"
Base leg to ground (the transistor side of the 4.7 kΩ)

What the numbers say

On: the collector reads ≈ 0.2 V — the transistor is almost a closed switch, almost a plain wire to ground. The base sits at ≈ 0.7 V: the base's own voltage drop — same idea as an LED's, smaller. Off: the collector reads high — but probably not the full 5 V; somewhere around 3.5 V is typical. Where did the rest go? Lesson 6 already showed you: a dark red LED still holds about a volt and a half. The meter itself lets a tiny trickle through the loop — far too little to light anything — and at that trickle the LED still takes its volt and a half. The rest piles up across the open switch. Nearly nothing across it when on, most of the supply when off: that's what a switch looks like to a meter.

9 The multiplier, sampled

How much bigger is the permitted current? The meter can ask: the dial position marked hFE and the little socket with lettered holes.

  1. Power off. Pull your transistor and seat it in the meter's NPN socket, each leg in the hole with its letter: E, B, C.
  2. Dial to hFE and read the number. Compare with the tables around you — same part number, same bin.
  3. Reseat it on the board — flat face toward you, E-B-C, said out loud — before the power comes back.

The numbers land anywhere from about 100 to 300. That's the gain — engineers write it β, "beta" — and each reading is one sample, taken at the meter's own tiny test current, not a constant of nature.

Design for the weakest

If β is a lottery, how does anyone design with it? Plan on the smallest number the bin might hold. Our switch feeds the base several times the minimum whisper a weak part would need — so every transistor in the bin slams fully on, and a strong β is a bonus, never a requirement.

10 The knob that decides

In lesson 5, the pot dimmed an LED smoothly, end to end. Feed the pot's wiper to the base instead and something different happens.

First, what a pot does with all three legs wired: it's lesson 5's divider in one part. The wiper splits the pot's 10 kΩ into a top half and a bottom half, and the bigger half takes the bigger share of the 5 volts — the rule from lesson 5 and lesson 6's warm-up. The wiper reads the bottom half's share: turn toward the + end, the bottom half grows, and the wiper climbs from 0 toward 5 V.

  1. Power off. Pot across the rails: outer legs to + and ground.
  2. Unplug the control wire. The wiper → the 4.7 kΩ's free row takes its place. Collector loop unchanged.
  3. Meter: black probe parked in ground, red on the wiper's row.
  4. Predict: turning the knob end to end — a smooth dim, like the dimmer? Power on, sweep slowly, and catch two numbers:

Most of the turn does nothing — dark, dark, dark — then the whole performance arrives in one narrow slice: a faint glow, a quick climb, full brightness. After that, more turning changes nothing again. Below the base's ≈ 0.7 V door, no whisper gets in. Inside the slice the transistor really is dimming — where the slice sits depends on your pot and your part. Past it, saturation: fully on, permission to spare. Where lesson 5's dimmer spread its change across the whole knob, this circuit crushes it into a sliver: almost off-or-on. A circuit that squeezes a smooth quantity toward yes-or-no is making a threshold decision — and your two numbers bracket this one's threshold, just above the base's 0.7 V. Done? Power off, pot out, control wire back.

11 The capacitor runs the show

Lesson 6's capacitor could light an LED for about a second. Watch what the transistor does for it. Get out your warm-up drawing — it's half of this build.

  1. Power off. Unplug the control wire (and the pot, if it's still in) — the base resistor's free row is about to get a new signal.
  2. On free rows beside the switch, build lesson 6's store-and-spend circuit from your warm-up drawing: beep-test both buttons first, and stripe to ground — say it.
  3. The baseline, the lesson 6 way: power on, hold ① for two seconds, let go, press ② and count the flash. About a second of light.
  4. Power off. One new wire: node A → the 4.7 kΩ's free row. Path ② stays; leave its button alone from here on.
  5. Power on. Predict first: what will the switch's LED do after you let go of ①, and for how long? Then hold ① for two seconds, let go, and count.

The LED holds on — ten seconds or more — then slowly fades. Same capacitor, same stored energy as the one-second flash you just counted. The difference: the switch's LED is paid for by the supply now, through the collector; the capacitor's store goes only to the base's whisper. A small store, trickled out slowly, commanding a big bright current: that is amplification, and you just watched it.

12 Challenge: tune the timer

The timer schematic one more time — but now it reads C = ▢, base resistor = ▢. Pick them so the LED stays on as close to five seconds as you can get after you let go of ①. Predict which direction each lever pushes before you swap anything. Closest table wins.

The finish line. The LED fades instead of snapping off, so eyes will argue about when it's "off." The meter settles it, like lesson 6's 3.2 V line: black probe in ground, red on the collector. It reads ≈ 0.2 V while the switch is fully on, and climbs as the switch lets go. Stop the clock when the collector crosses 2.5 V — the switch half open. Meter partners split the jobs: one lets go of ① and counts; the other watches the meter and calls "two-point-five!"

Hold ① for two seconds before every run — it refills the capacitor, so every run starts from the same place. Swapping capacitors? Reset the one you pull out before it goes in the box.

13 More to try

For the fast and the curious

1. Blue in the collector loop. Swap the red LED for blue. Predict the collector-to-ground reading when on, and the brightness, before you look. 2. Other base resistors. In the knob build, replace the 4.7 kΩ with 1 kΩ, then 10 kΩ — where does the glow-to-full slice move? (In the timer, that's the challenge's second lever.) 3. Push to light, let go to dark. Replace the control wire with a pushbutton — + rail → button (the two legs that beep only while pressed) → the 4.7 kΩ's free row. Predict pressed, then released. Released is the dangling wire all over again: no command. Fix it with a 10 kΩ from that row to the ground rail. Now letting go means off — told, not hoped. That resistor is a pull-down, and digital circuits put one — or its mirror image, a pull-up — on nearly every button. (Pressed, the button ties the row straight to +, and the 10 kΩ just carries a harmless half-milliamp to ground.) 4. Bold move: make yourself the base resistor — control wire in one hand, a jumper from the + rail pinched in the other. Hand to hand, you're hundreds of kΩ: a legal base resistor, and the glow may be faint. (A Darlington pair — two transistors stacked so their gains multiply — makes this trick effortless; ask us about it.)

The math, for the curious — always optional

Your switch by the numbers. Fully on, the loop drops 2.0 V (LED) + 0.2 V (transistor), leaving 2.8 V across the 220 Ω:

I=2.8 V220 Ω≈13 mA

The minimum permission, at a weak β of 100:

13 mAβ=13 mA100=0.13 mA

What the 4.7 kΩ actually delivers:

5−0.74700≈0.9 mA

— about seven times that minimum, on purpose. The surplus is why the whole bin works: the switch saturates whether your β is 100 or 300. The Transistor's Arithmetic sheet on the math shelf runs the whole design, including how the 4.7 kΩ was chosen.

Words from Part 2

gain (β)
the permission multiplier: one unit of base current permits up to β units of collector current (the meter's hFE socket reads one sample of it)
saturation
fully on — the base permits more than the collector loop can draw, and the transistor drops only ≈ 0.2 V, as close to a closed switch as it gets
threshold
the input level where a circuit stops dimming and starts deciding
amplification
a small signal commanding a bigger one — here, a capacitor's trickle into the base running a bright LED from the supply
pull-down
a resistor from an input to ground, so "no command" still means off
