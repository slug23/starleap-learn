P = IV

The formula

P = I × V. Power equals current times voltage drop — measured in watts (W), the rate energy is being delivered. Every part in a circuit is converting energy at some number of watts, right now.

What power is

Energy is the stuff (measured in joules); power is how fast it moves (joules per second = watts). A part with current flowing through it and voltage dropping across it is taking energy out of the circuit at P = I × V watts — the LED turns its share into light, the resistor turns its share into heat. (Why the units multiply out so neatly is on the What's a Coulomb? sheet — it's arithmetic, not coincidence.)

What gets used up is energy, not current. Every milliamp that flows into a part flows back out the other side. What the charge leaves behind is energy: it arrives at a part at a higher voltage and leaves at a lower one, and that voltage drop is exactly how much energy each coulomb gave up there. Current counts the coulombs passing each second; the voltage drop is the energy each one gives up; multiply them and you have the power.

Get the units straight first

The formula works when the units agree: watts with amps and volts. Our currents are milliamps, so convert before plugging in: 14 mA = 0.014 A. Our powers come out small, too: 1 W = 1,000 mW, so a ¼ W rating is 250 mW.

The mA · mW shortcut

Leave the current in milliamps and the power comes out in milliwatts — the milli carries straight through: mA × V = mW, mW ÷ V = mA, mW ÷ mA = V. Example: 14 mA through a part with a 2.0 V drop is 14 × 2.0 = 28 mW. (The Milli, Kilo, Mega sheet covers the prefixes.)

Worked: the energy books of one LED loop

The classic loop — 5 V supply, red LED (2.0 V drop), 220 Ω resistor (3.0 V drop), 14 mA everywhere:

PartP = I × VBecomes
LED14 mA × 2.0 V = 28 mWlight (and a little warmth)
Resistor14 mA × 3.0 V = 42 mWheat, on purpose
Supply delivers14 mA × 5.0 V = 70 mW28 + 42 ✓ — the books balance

Notice the quiet scandal: the resistor throws away more power than the LED uses. That's the price of the simple circuit — the resistor's job is to burn off the extra push as heat, and 42 mW of heat is so little you'll never feel it.

Which V?

Look at what went into each row: the V is the voltage drop across that part, not the supply's 5 V. The LED's power uses the LED's 2.0 V; the resistor's uses the resistor's 3.0 V. The full 5 V goes in only for a part sitting straight across the rails, or for the supply itself. Reaching for 5 V every time is the mistake nearly everyone makes once.

Why kit resistors have a rating

Our resistors are rated ¼ W = 250 mW — the most heat they can shed before they cook. The classic loop's 42 mW isn't close. But watch what happens as resistance falls across the full 5 V:

Straight across 5 VCurrent (I = V ÷ R)Power (P = I × V)Verdict
1 kΩ5 mA25 mWcool
220 Ω≈ 23 mA≈ 114 mWwarm, fine
100 Ω50 mA250 mWat the limit — hot enough to hurt a fingertip
a bare wire (≈ 0 Ω)as much as the supply can pushall the supply can deliver, as heat — in the wire and in the supply itselfthis is a short — this is why shorts burn

Smaller resistance means more current and the same voltage — power climbs fast. A short circuit is the end of that story: the largest current the supply can push, and nowhere for the power to go but heat — in a wire that was never meant to shed any, and inside the supply straining to deliver it. That is how one stray wire burns a finger and damages a supply.

Power over time: how long will it last?

Power is a rate, so multiply it by time and you get the energy spent: energy = power × time, or joules = watts × seconds. The classic loop takes 70 mW, which is 0.07 J every second.

A fresh AA battery holds roughly 10,000 J (the What's a Coulomb? sheet again). That is enough energy to keep the classic loop lit for 10,000 ÷ 0.07 ≈ 143,000 seconds — about 40 hours.

Joules are small, so batteries and electric bills use a bigger unit built the same way: the watt-hour (Wh), one watt for one hour, or 3,600 J. A phone battery holds about 15 Wh. The electric company sells energy by the kilowatt-hour (kWh): a 1,000 W microwave running for a full hour uses exactly one.

For scale

  • Your LED: 0.028 W
  • A phone charger at 5 V × 3 A: 15 W
  • An old incandescent bulb: 60 W — over 2,000 of your LEDs
  • A microwave: ≈ 1,000 W
  • Everything one household outlet circuit will supply before its breaker trips: 120 V × 15 A = 1,800 W
  • A fast charger filling an electric car: ≈ 150,000 W (150 kW)
  • One rack of AI computers in a datacenter: over 100,000 W today, with 1,000,000 W (1 MW) designs announced

Two more forms of the same formula

P = I × V needs a current and a voltage. For a resistor, Ohm's law (V = IR) ties those two together, so either one can be traded away. Getting there takes a little algebra; using the results is plain arithmetic.

SwapYou getReach for it when
V for I × RP = I² × Ryou know the current through something — above all, a wire
I for V ÷ RP = V² ÷ Ryou know the voltage across a resistor

I² only means I × I. Check both against the 100 Ω row of the rating table — 5 V across it, 50 mA (0.05 A) through it: 0.05 × 0.05 × 100 = 0.25 W, and 5 × 5 ÷ 100 = 0.25 W. The same 250 mW, three ways.

The second form is that whole rating table in one line: with the voltage fixed, halve the resistance and the power doubles.

The I²R story: why long wires want high voltage

No wire is a perfect conductor. Every cable has a little resistance, and by P = I² × R it turns some of the power passing through it into heat — wasted before it arrives. The square is what matters: double the current and the wire wastes four times as much.

Now use P = I × V on whatever sits at the far end. It needs a certain power, and that power can arrive as a large current at a low voltage or a small current at a high voltage — the product is the same either way. The wire in between only notices the current. Deliver 1,000 W through a cable with 0.1 Ω of resistance:

Deliver 1,000 W atCurrent needed (I = P ÷ V)Wasted in the cable (I² × R)
10 V100 A100 × 100 × 0.1 = 1,000 W — as much as arrives
100 V10 A10 × 10 × 0.1 = 10 W
1,000 V1 A1 × 1 × 0.1 = 0.1 W

Each time the voltage goes up ten-fold, the current drops ten-fold and the waste drops a hundred-fold. That one table is why anything that moves serious power over a distance does it at the highest voltage it can manage.

Now you know why

  • Why "never make a short" is rule 1: a short leaves the supply's power nowhere to go but heat. And the wire's tiny resistance is no protection — I² × R with an enormous I is still a hot wire.
  • Why a transistor switch runs cool: fully on, it has only ≈ 0.2 V across it, so it takes 0.2 V × 13 mA ≈ 2.6 mW while the LED it controls gets nearly everything. The Transistor's Arithmetic sheet shows what happens when it is only half on.
  • Why a phone's fast charger raises its voltage: the cable is thin and can carry only a few amps, so past 15 W the charger and the phone agree on 9 V or more. More volts, the same amps, more watts.
  • Why the power grid runs at hundreds of thousands of volts: the cable table above, at the scale of a city. The How AC Works page, under the hood, tells that story.
  • Why datacenters are moving from 54 V to 800 V: one rack of AI computers can need a million watts. The Careers in DC Power report follows that one through.

Practice

Answers at the bottom. The currents come from V = IR — that sheet first if these feel unfair.

  1. The blue-LED loop: 3.2 V drop, 8 mA. What power does the blue LED take?
  2. Same loop: the 220 Ω resistor has the other 1.8 V. Its power?
  3. A 1 kΩ resistor sits straight across the 5 V rails. Power? Is ¼ W enough?
  4. What resistance straight across 5 V would hit the ¼ W limit exactly? (Two steps: I = P ÷ V first, then R = V ÷ I.)
  5. A part takes 28 mW while 14 mA flows through it. What is the voltage drop across it — and which part of the classic loop is it?
  6. Redo problem 3 with P = V² ÷ R, never finding the current. Same answer?
  7. A cable with 0.1 Ω of resistance carries 20 A. How much power does the cable itself waste? Now the same power is sent at double the voltage — what does the cable waste?
  8. A phone battery holds about 15 Wh. For how many hours could it keep the classic 70 mW loop lit?

Check the books with the meter

The worked example is buildable: red LED, 220 Ω, 5 V. Measure three voltages — across the LED, across the resistor, and rail to rail. Get the current the engineer's way, from the resistor: its drop ÷ 0.22 kΩ gives milliamps. Then work out the LED's power, the resistor's, and the supply's (the rail voltage × that same current). If LED + resistor lands within a few milliwatts of the supply's figure, the books balance on your bench and not just on this page.

Answers

1. 8 mA × 3.2 V ≈ 26 mW. 2. 8 mA × 1.8 V ≈ 14 mW — this loop wastes less than the red one; the blue LED claims more of the 5 V. 3. I = 5 mA, P = 25 mW — a tenth of the rating; fine. 4. In shortcut units: I = 250 mW ÷ 5 V = 50 mA, then R = 5 V ÷ 50 mA = 0.1 kΩ = 100 Ω. That is the edge, with no margin at all — the kit's smallest resistor, 220 Ω, runs at under half its rating in the same spot.

5. V = P ÷ I = 28 mW ÷ 14 mA = 2.0 V — the red LED. 6. 5 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 0.025 W = 25 mW. Same answer, one step. 7. 20 × 20 × 0.1 = 40 W. At double the voltage the current halves to 10 A: 10 × 10 × 0.1 = 10 W — a quarter of the waste. 8. 15 Wh ÷ 0.07 W ≈ 214 hours — almost nine days.
