Sound & the Light Theremin

The big idea

A sound is something moving back and forth hundreds of times a second. Feed the 555's flips to a speaker and each flip moves its cone; flip fast enough and the clicks become a tone. The resistor and capacitor that set the blink's tempo now set the pitch — more ohms or a bigger capacitor, a lower note. Put lesson 9's LDR in a resistor's place, and the light in the room plays the pitch.

Part 1

The basics

One new part, one build — and you already built most of it. Every step today is a single change to lesson 10's blinker, and you predict before each one. This part is the lesson: finish it and you've done the day's work. Part 2, further down, is extra.

3 Warm-up: the blinker, from memory

Kits closed, one minute: draw lesson 10's blinker from memory — the 555 as a box, the pin numbers where wires land, both resistors, the capacitor, the LED. Pin numbers matter more than where they sit on the box. Then open the box: the blinker is standing where you left it. Check it against your drawing, power on, and count one blink. (Box lost it, or it never blinked? Build it now from your drawing — notch first, power after.) Keep it standing. Every step today is one change to it.

4 New component: the speaker

Inside a speaker is a coil of thin wire sitting next to a magnet, glued to a paper or plastic cone. Current through the coil makes it a magnet too, so it pushes against the fixed magnet and moves the cone. Current off, the cone springs back. Cone out, cone back: the air in front of it gets squeezed and released, and your ear reads that as sound.

The coil is nearly a short

That coil is a few ohms — about 8 Ω. On its own across 5 V it would let through more than half an amp: hot wires, and a 555 driven far past its limit. So the speaker always gets a 220 Ω in series to limit the current — the same job the 220 Ω does for an LED. Never wire a speaker straight to pin 3 or across the rails.

5 Hear the blink

  1. Power off. 220 Ω from pin 3's row to an empty row.
  2. Speaker from that row to the ground rail — one lead in the row, the other in the rail.
  3. Trace it: pin 3 → 220 Ω → speaker → ground. The speaker never touches pin 3 directly.

Predict

The blinker still flips about once a second. What will the speaker do?

Power on and listen close. A tick when the LED lights, a softer one when it goes dark — every flip moves the cone. That's all a speaker can do: move when the current changes. One flip a second is a click. So what happens if the flips come faster?

6 From clicks to a tone

Lesson 10 left you a cliffhanger: the little 100 nF disc in place of the 100 µF made the LED blink about seven hundred times a second — too fast for your eye, too fast for the meter. Your ears are faster.

  1. Power off. Pull the 100 µF and give it the reset before it goes in the box — bare 220 Ω across its legs for a slow count of three.
  2. The 100 nF disc into the same two rows: pin 6's row and the ground rail. It has no stripe — either way around.

Predict

Seven hundred clicks every second. What will you hear?

Power on. A steady tone. It's the same click, seven hundred times a second — too close together for your ear to hear separately. How many flips happen each second is the signal's frequency, measured in hertz (Hz): one hertz is one flip-and-back per second, so your tone is about 700 Hz. Your ear hears frequency as pitch — more hertz, higher note. People hear from about 20 Hz to about 20,000 Hz. And the LED? Look at it: a steady half-brightness. It's still blinking seven hundred times a second; the speaker is the only thing on the board that can keep up.

Twenty speakers, one room

When a teacher raises a hand, power off — the whole room goes quiet at once. Power back on when the hand comes down.

7 Change the pitch

In lesson 10 the 10 kΩ — between pin 7 and pin 6 — set how fast the capacitor charged and discharged: more resistance, slower charging, slower blink. Same rule now, but you'll hear it instead of timing it. One swap at a time, power off for each, and predict before you listen:

The 100 kΩ drops the note to a low buzz — about 70 flips a second, slow enough that you can almost hear the separate clicks again. The 4.7 kΩ sends it up to about twice your starting pitch. More ohms, slower flips, lower note. It's the blink-tempo rule from lesson 10, now played instead of timed. Put the 10 kΩ back.

8 The light theremin

A theremin is a musical instrument you play without touching it — the player's hands move in the air near it, and the pitch follows. Yours will follow the light. You already own the part: lesson 9's LDR, a resistor whose resistance changes with light.

  1. Power off. Pull the 10 kΩ. LDR into the same two rows — pin 7's row and pin 6's row — face up, where light can land.

Predict

You cup your hand over the disc. Does the note go up or down — and why? Commit before you move:

Power on and play it. Room light: the LDR is a few thousand ohms, somewhere near the 4.7 kΩ — a fairly high note. Cup it: the LDR swells toward a hundred thousand ohms and the note sinks into a buzz; seal it tight and it slows all the way back down into separate clicks. Lift your hand slowly and the pitch climbs back up. Same three steps as the night-light: the LDR is the input, the 555 does the processing, the speaker is the output — but this output isn't on or off, it follows your hand the whole way.

Part 1 is done when…

  • You heard the slow blink as clicks, then the 100 nF turn them into a tone.
  • You changed the pitch with a resistor swap and predicted which way it would go.
  • Your light theremin plays — and you can say why covering the LDR lowers the note.

All three? That's the lesson — you built a musical instrument out of a timer. The words and the exit ticket are right below; Part 2 comes after them, if you want more.

9 Words to know

speaker
a coil, a magnet and a cone: current through the coil moves the cone, and the cone moves the air
frequency
how many times a signal flips and comes back each second
hertz (Hz)
the unit of frequency: one hertz is once per second; your tone was about 700 Hz
pitch
how high or low a sound is — what your ear makes of frequency
theremin
an instrument played without touching it; yours is played with light

12 Challenge: the doorbell

Spec, no recipe: the tone generator plays one note, and a different note for as long as you hold a pushbutton. Draw it first, then build it. Your parts: the standing build, a pushbutton (beep-test it for the pair of legs that connects only while pressed), and a second 10 kΩ. Two decisions are yours: where the button and the second resistor go, and whether pressing should raise the note or lower it — predict which your drawing does before you power on. Lesson 3 has a clue: two resistors side by side between the same two rows let more through than one.

Drawing only

This build has no numbered steps — your drawing is the whole recipe. Nothing else changes: one row = one node, power off while you rewire, and the speaker keeps its 220 Ω. Read, wire, verify.

13 More to try

For the fast and the curious

1. One octave down: borrow a neighbor's 100 nF and seat it right beside yours — same two rows. Predict the note. Twice the capacitance halves the frequency, and musicians have a name for a note at exactly half the frequency: the same note, one octave lower. Hum along and check. 2. Tame the theremin: back on the theremin, put a 4.7 kΩ in series with the LDR. Predict what happens to the highest note in bright light. Then try a 100 kΩ beside the LDR instead (same two rows) — what happens to the lowest note when you cup it? You're choosing your instrument's range. 3. The duet: two tables, two theremins, one song. Who plays the low part? 4. Louder: trade the speaker's 220 Ω for a 100 Ω (brown · black · brown). Predict the volume. Then, power off, touch the 100 Ω — warm or cool? Never go below 100 Ω.

The math, for the curious — always optional

Lesson 10's blink formula gave the time for one full blink. Frequency is blinks per second — the time's reciprocal, 1 divided by it — which works out to:

f=1.44(R1+2·R2)×C

With R1 = 1 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ and the 100 nF disc:

f=1.4421000 Ω×0.0000001 F≈690 Hz

Swap R2 for 100 kΩ and the bottom becomes 201,000 × 0.0000001 — about ten times bigger — so the frequency falls to about 70 Hz. With the 4.7 kΩ it climbs to about 1,400 Hz. Double the capacitor and the bottom doubles: half the frequency, one octave down. The 1.44 is 1 divided by lesson 10's 0.693.
