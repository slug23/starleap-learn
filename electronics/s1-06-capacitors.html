Capacitors

The big idea

A capacitor stores charge — and filling or emptying it through a resistor takes time. Today, time becomes something your circuits control.

1 Today you will…

  • Charge a capacitor, disconnect the supply, and light an LED from stored charge.
  • Watch a capacitor fill in slow motion on the meter — and time it with a stopwatch.
  • Race fills and fades: predict how resistor and capacitor sizes change the clock.
  • Tune a fill to hit a five-second target.

2 Your kit today

  • Board with supply on the rails — cleared to bare rails last lesson — plus jumpers, LEDs, 220 Ω, resistor assortment, multimeter
  • Two big capacitors — 1000 µF, the cans with a stripe
  • One middle capacitor — 100 µF, a smaller can
  • One tiny capacitor — 100 nF ceramic, the little disc
  • A way to count seconds — a timer if you have one; steady one-Mississippis if you don't (precision isn't the point yet)

3 Warm-up: divider from memory

No peeking at lesson 5: build a 1 kΩ / 1 kΩ voltage divider across the rails, say your node letters out loud, predict the tap voltage, then measure it. Black probe parked in the ground rail, as always. Done when your meter agrees with your prediction.

4 New component: the capacitor

Two metal plates, face to face, separated by an insulator. Charge can't cross the gap — so when the supply pushes, charge piles up on the plates instead. Think of it as a bucket for charge: this lesson fills it, empties it, and times both. From here on, the correct words: a capacitor charges and discharges, and how much it holds is its capacitance, measured in farads — ours are marked in µF (microfarads) and nF (nanofarads), because a whole farad is enormous.

Polarity — check it before every power-up

The can capacitors are electrolytic: they have a + and a − side, and they mean it. Wired backwards, an electrolytic slowly cooks itself and can burst its safety vent. House rule for this whole course: the striped leg always plugs toward ground — and you check the stripe before connecting power, every single build. The house rule works because ground is always our lowest voltage; the rule beneath it, for circuits beyond this course: striped leg to the lower-voltage side, and never more volts than the rating printed on the can. (The little ceramic disc has no polarity; either way round is fine.)

5 Charge it, then light it

First contact: make light from a part with no battery in it.

  1. Plug a 1000 µF capacitor into the board: + leg in one row (call it node A), striped leg in another row jumpered to the ground rail. Stripe check.
  2. Run a 220 Ω resistor from the + rail to node A. Power on. Count two seconds — the capacitor is charging. Meter check: node A reads ≈ 5 V.
  3. Disconnect the charging path — pull the resistor's rail end out. The supply is now out of the story. Predict: what does the meter read at node A? Check.
  4. Now touch an LED + 220 Ω chain from node A to ground (long LED leg toward A). Predict first — then watch.

A flash, then a fade, then dark. Where did that light come from? From the energy you parked on the plates. The supply crowded charge onto the capacitor; when you opened a path, that charge flowed back around the loop, and the LED turned the stored energy into light and a little heat. The charge itself wasn't eaten — it moved. The energy is what got spent. That's a capacitor's whole career: store now, spend later.

6 Watch it fill

Charging through 220 Ω was too fast to see. Swap in a big resistor and the fill goes slow motion — slow enough to time:

  1. Rebuild the charge circuit with 10 kΩ instead of 220. Meter on node A.
  2. Drain first: before every timed run, pull the charging path out, then hold a bare 220 Ω — no LED — across the capacitor, node A to ground, until the meter reads under 0.1 V. Two seconds does it. Take the drain resistor back out before the run. A fair race starts empty — and an LED chain can't get you there, as §7 is about to prove.
  3. Connect the charging path and start counting. Stop the clock when the meter crosses 3.2 V — that's today's finish line, same for every race. Write the time down.

Watch the numbers as it fills: fast at first, then slower and slower — the fuller it gets, the weaker the push across the resistor, the slower the pour. It creeps toward 5 V and never quite arrives. (Why 3.2 V is the fair finish line is a secret the math box spills.)

Predict, then race

Your 10 kΩ / 1000 µF time is the baseline. Predict each of these before you build it — more resistance means a slower pour; a smaller capacitor is a smaller bucket:

7 The drain race

Fades obey the same clock, run backwards. Charge the 1000 µF fully (through 220, quick), disconnect the supply, then discharge into a red LED chain and watch the light die (red for everyone — you'll compare notes at the end):

  1. Race 1: red LED + 220 Ω. Watch the fade; count its length.
  2. Race 2: recharge, then the same red LED + 1 kΩ. Predict first: brighter or dimmer? Longer or shorter?

The 1 kΩ drain is dimmer and slower — less current is less light and a gentler pour. Now the meter reveal: when the light has completely died, measure the capacitor. Predict first.

The fade dies, the charge doesn't

The meter still reads well over a volt. Write your number down — the table next to you will have a slightly different one, and both are right: no two LEDs give up at exactly the same point. The capacitor isn't empty; its voltage has just sunk to where the red LED passes too little current for your eyes to catch, so the fade outlived your looking. Dark doesn't mean drained: one more reason the meter, not your eyes, gets the last word.

8 Why the flash always ends

One more build, one sharp idea. Wire supply → 220 Ω → LED → capacitor → ground — the capacitor in the loop, stripe to ground. Predict: steady light, or something else?

A flash, then dark — while the power is still on. Current flowed only while charge was piling onto the plates; once the capacitor filled, the pileup stopped, and so did the current. A capacitor passes current only while its voltage is changing. Steady current can't cross the gap — remember this sentence; the digital semester is built on it.

9 Challenge: the five-second fill

The fill circuit one more time — but now the schematic reads R = ▢, C = ▢. Pick them from your kit so the fill from 0 to 3.2 V takes as close to five seconds as you can get. Series and parallel resistor tricks are legal. Predict from your race table, drain properly, build, time it, adjust. Closest table wins.

Go further — for the fast and the curious

1. Borrow a neighbor's 10 kΩ and refill through 10 kΩ + 10 kΩ in series — predict the time first. Twice the squeeze, twice the…? 2. Plug both 1000 µF capacitors in parallel and rerun the baseline fill. Capacitance adds in parallel — two buckets side by side — the reverse of what resistors did. 3. The pocket flashlight: charge a 1000 µF, pull it gently from the board, walk it across the room, and light a neighbor's LED. 4. Try the tiny 100 nF disc in the fill circuit. Nothing visible? It's 10,000× smaller — some buckets are thimbles. It'll get its real job beside a chip, soon. 5. Make alternating current with your bare hands: plug two LEDs in anti-parallel (second one reversed) behind one 220 Ω, then swap the supply leads back and forth, steadily. The LEDs take turns — you're generating AC at about 1 Hz. One part is banned from this game: the electrolytics — a supply that keeps reversing is exactly what the cans can't survive. (How capacitors and AC actually get along is on the How AC Works page, Under the hood.)

The math, for the curious — always optional

The secret of 3.2 V: multiply resistance by capacitance and you get seconds:

τ=R×C=10 kΩ×1000 µF=10 s

— and that product is exactly how long the fill takes to reach 63% of the supply. 63% of 5 V ≈ 3.2 V: your stopwatch was measuring τ all along. (Baseline landed at 8 or 12 seconds instead of 10? Blame the can, not your clock — electrolytics commonly run ±20% from their printed value.) Check it against your race table, then read the τ = RC sheet on the math shelf for the whole story.

10 Words to know

capacitor
two plates and a gap — a component that stores charge
capacitance
how much it holds, measured in farads: µF and nF in our kit
charge / discharge
filling the plates / spending what's stored
electrolytic capacitor
the big polarized cans — stripe marks the − leg, and it matters
ceramic capacitor
the tiny disc — small, fast, no polarity
polarized
a part with a + and − that must face the right way

11 Exit ticket

On your card: 1. draw the charge circuit and the LED discharge circuit from memory (schematic symbols, stripe side marked). 2. One sentence: where did the energy for the light come from after the supply was disconnected? Hand it in at the door.

Then the teardown ritual: hold the bare 220 Ω across every capacitor until the meter calls it empty. Parts go back in the box asleep.
