The Blinker

The big idea

Wire a circuit's output back around to its own input and it can run itself. Two transistor switches, each set up to shut the other one off through a capacitor, can never settle — they trade turns forever, with no hand on anything. The loop is called feedback, and the capacitor's slow refill is the clock.

Part 1

The basics

No new parts today — a new arrangement of four you already own: resistor, capacitor, LED, transistor. Lesson 7's switch gets a twin, and the two get wired to each other. This part is the lesson: finish it and you've done the day's work. Part 2, further down, is extra.

Check your drawing

Now open the box: the switch is standing where you left it. Check it against your drawing, then prove both commands — control wire to the + rail: on; to the ground rail: off. (Box lost it, or never finished it? Build it now from the drawing — flat face toward you, E-B-C.) Keep the drawing out. It's the plan for the next build.

4 Build the twin

Your drawing is a complete recipe, and you've followed it once already. Follow it again: a second switch, on free rows beside the first. From here on the standing switch is switch 1 and its twin is switch 2; their LEDs are LED 1 and LED 2.

  1. Power off. Second transistor into three free rows — flat face toward you, E · B · C, said out loud. Leave four or five empty rows between the two transistors; two capacitors are going to need the space.
  2. Build the rest from your drawing: + rail → 220 Ω → LED (long leg toward +) → collector row; emitter row → ground rail; 4.7 kΩ from the base row to an empty row; a control wire out of that row.
  3. Before power: trace each base with a finger. Its only way in is through its own 4.7 kΩ.
  4. Plug both control wires into the + rail and power on. Both LEDs light: two switches, both told on.
  5. Predict, then test: pull switch 1's control wire and touch it to the ground rail. What does LED 2 do? Then park the wire back in the + rail.

LED 1 goes dark, and LED 2 never notices. Right now these are two separate circuits that happen to share a board — neither switch knows the other exists. Two capacitors are about to change that.

5 Cross the switches

Look at a switch's collector row — where the LED's chain meets the transistor. While the switch is off, that row rests a few volts up. When the switch turns on, it drops to nearly zero. So the collector is the switch's output: its voltage reports what the switch is doing. The base is its input: where it takes commands. Today's move is to connect each switch's output to the other switch's input — through a capacitor.

The stripe rule, grown up

Until today every capacitor's stripe went to ground, because ground was always the lower-voltage side. The real rule is the one underneath: the stripe leg goes to the lower voltage. In this build neither leg touches ground. The + leg lands on a collector, which rests a few volts up; the stripe lands on a base, which never climbs past 0.7 V. So today's line, said out loud before power: "plus to collector, stripe to base."

  1. Power off. Both control wires stay parked in the + rail.
  2. First capacitor: + leg (the long one) into switch 1's collector row; stripe leg into switch 2's base row. Legs won't reach? Don't force them — seat the capacitor across two empty rows and run a jumper from each row to where it belongs. A jumper makes two rows one node.
  3. Second capacitor, the mirror image: + leg into switch 2's collector row; stripe leg into switch 1's base row.
  4. Before power: trace each capacitor on the board with a finger — it starts at one switch's collector and ends at the other switch's base. Then say it, once per capacitor: "plus to collector, stripe to base."

Predict

(Not trading? Power off and run the Debugging Loop. The usual suspects: a capacitor leg one row off, a capacitor in backwards, or a control wire that came out of the + rail.)

Why it can't sit still

The 4.7 kΩs never stopped telling both switches on. So what keeps shutting them off? Follow one handoff:

  1. Switch 1 turns on. Its collector drops — from a few volts to nearly zero.
  2. The capacitor on that collector can't change its charge in an instant; lesson 6 showed you that filling and emptying take time. So when its + leg drops, its stripe leg drops by the same amount — and pulls switch 2's base down with it, far below the 0.7 V a base needs. Switch 2 is shut off, whatever its 4.7 kΩ says.
  3. Now that 4.7 kΩ goes to work, refilling the capacitor's low side from the + rail: lesson 6's slow fill. Switch 2's base creeps back up. This is the wait you just counted.
  4. The base reaches 0.7 V. Switch 2 turns on, its collector drops — and the second capacitor does to switch 1 exactly what was just done to switch 2.

Around and around. Each switch, the moment it turns on, shuts the other off; each one that's shut off is back a couple of seconds later to return the favor. Neither can stay on, neither can stay off — and nobody outside the circuit is giving orders. Each switch's output has become the other's input, so what the circuit does loops back to decide what it does next. That loop is feedback. A circuit that feedback keeps flipping, for as long as it has power, is an oscillator. And its tempo is step 3: a resistor filling a capacitor.

Part 1 is done when…

  • Your two LEDs trade turns on their own — hands off the board.
  • On the schematic, you can trace each capacitor from one switch's collector to the other switch's base.
  • You can say what shuts each switch off, and what brings it back on.

All three? That's the lesson — you built a circuit that runs itself. The words and the exit ticket are right below; Part 2 comes after them, if you want more.

6 Words to know

output · input
where a stage reports what it's doing · where it takes its commands — for the transistor switch, the collector and the base
feedback
a circuit's output wired back around to its own input, so what it does now decides what it does next
oscillator
a circuit that feedback keeps flipping between two states, with nobody commanding it
cross-coupled
two switches wired so each one's output feeds the other's input

8 Below zero

Black probe parked in ground, red probe on a bare wire stub in switch 2's base row, meter on DC volts. Predict first: the supply makes 0 V and 5 V and nothing else — so what's the lowest number this row can show?

Now watch a few full turns and catch three readings:

The momentSwitch 2's base reads
Just after LED 2 loses its turn
Just before LED 2 comes back on
While LED 2 is lit

What the numbers say

A minus sign — around −2.5 V, on a board whose supply only makes 0 to 5. Nothing is broken and the meter is right. That's step 2 of the handoff, caught in the act: the capacitor was sitting charged, + side a few volts above its stripe side. When switch 1 turned on and pulled the + side down to nearly zero, the stripe side had to land a few volts below it — below ground itself. Then the climb: the 4.7 kΩ refills it, the reading rises through zero, and right around 0.7 V — the base's door from lesson 7 — the switch snaps on, and the number parks there until the next handoff.

One more: red probe on switch 2's collector row. Nearly zero while LED 2 is lit. When its turn ends the collector climbs back — fast at first, then slower and slower, lesson 6's fill curve again — and tops out near 3.3 V, not 5. The missing volt and a half is lesson 6's leftover voltage: a dark red LED still holds about that much.

9 What sets the tempo

Step 3 of the handoff was a resistor filling a capacitor, and lesson 6 gave you the rule for that: more ohms, slower fill. So the base resistors should set the tempo. Predict each row, then swap and time it. Power off for every swap.

Base resistorsPredictLED 1's turnLED 2's turn
Both 4.7 kΩ — your build— (baseline)
Both 10 kΩ
Switch 2's stays 10 kΩ; switch 1's goes back to 4.7 kΩwhose turn is longer?

About twice the ohms, about twice the wait: with 10 kΩ on both bases, each turn stretches to five or six seconds. The mixed row is the one worth arguing about. The 10 kΩ on switch 2's base stretches LED 1's turn, not LED 2's — because a base resistor times how long its own switch stays off, and while switch 2 is off, LED 1 is the one that's lit. Each base resistor sets the other LED's turn.

11 More to try

For the fast and the curious

1. The speed limit. Back on the plain blinker, make both base resistors 1 kΩ. Predict the turn — then look closely: the LEDs never go fully dark. Now put the 4.7 kΩs back and watch one slow handoff: the LED that just lost its turn doesn't snap off, it fades for about a second. What's lighting it? Trace the path on the drawing — + rail → 220 Ω → LED → capacitor → the other switch's base. The fade is that capacitor refilling, and its refill current runs through the LED. At 1 kΩ the turns are shorter than the refill. 2. Lopsided. Trade the capacitor on switch 1's collector for the 100 µF — reset the 1000 µF you pull, and say the stripe line. Predict which LED's turn shrinks, and by about how much, before you power on. 3. Freeze. A jumper from switch 1's base row, touched to the ground rail and held. Predict which LED stays lit. Then let go — who gets the next turn, and how soon? Ground only: a base touched straight to the + rail is lesson 7's cooked transistor. 4. A color changes the beat. Trade LED 1 for the blue one. One LED's turn gets shorter — predict which, then explain it. (Lesson 4's clue: blue takes 3.2 V of the supply for itself. How far can that collector fall now?)

The math, for the curious — always optional

The turn has a formula. Lesson 6's trick first — ohms × farads = seconds:

R×C=4700 Ω×0.001 F=4.7 s

One LED's turn is a bit more than half of that:

turn≈0.55×R×C≈2.6 s

Why 0.55? The base isn't filling from empty to full. It starts about 2.6 V below zero, heads for 5 V, and gets cut off at 0.7 V, the moment its switch turns on. That slice of lesson 6's fill curve costs about 0.55 of one RC. A whole beat is two turns, one timed by each base resistor:

beat≈0.55×(R1+R2)×C

Check it against section 9's table: 10 kΩ gives 0.55 × 10 s = 5.5 s a turn, and the knob's 1 kΩ-to-11 kΩ sweep runs from about half a second to about six. Textbooks print 0.69 for this circuit; ours comes out smaller because the LED in each collector keeps the collector from swinging the full 5 V. (Electrolytics run ±20%, so your stopwatch gets the last word.) The τ = RC sheet on the math shelf draws the curve all of this lives on.
