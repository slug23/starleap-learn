The Loop

Big idea

Electricity only flows around a complete, unbroken loop — and a circuit is nothing but components joined at connection points called nodes.

1 Today you will…

  • Light an LED held together by nothing but finger-pinches — safe to hold at our low 5 volts.
  • Read your first schematic — the "recipe drawing" for a circuit.
  • Explain what a circuit is (and why a broken loop does nothing).
  • Discover which everyday objects conduct electricity — and which don't.

2 Your kit today

  • Power supply — 5 V, with one red (+) and one black (−) wire coming out
  • A partner — the first build takes four hands
  • LED (red) — plus a spare
  • Resistor 220 Ω (red-red-brown stripes)
  • Mystery-object test bin (shared with your table)
  • Debugging Loop card — lives in your kit all year
  • Pencil — there are blanks to fill in

Safety — two rules

1. Never make a short circuit. If red touches black with no component in between — through a jumper, a pinch, anything — the supply's full strength dumps through that one path. The wire can get finger-burning hot in seconds and melt its insulation, and the power supply itself can be damaged. Bare metal touches only what you chose.

2. Never power an LED without its resistor. A "naked" LED draws far more current than it can survive. It doesn't just quietly die — it can pop like a tiny firecracker and throw hot plastic bits across the table. This really happens. The resistor is the LED's bodyguard: every LED, every circuit, no exceptions. And don't hover face-down over a circuit the first time you power it.

One comfort: at 5 volts, dry skin lets through only a whisper of current — far too little to feel — which is why you can hold today's live circuit in your bare fingers. The real hazard in this kit is indirect: rule 1's short circuit makes wires hot enough to burn.

And one hard line: house wiring is a different world. The 120 volts in wall outlets, cords, and light sockets can push enough current through a body to be fatal. Nothing in this class ever touches household wiring — and neither do you. Everything we build lives on the low-voltage side, full stop.

3 Meet the components

Every component has a symbol. Symbols are how circuit builders write things down — like sheet music for electricity.

4 How to read a schematic

A schematic is the recipe for a circuit. Three rules:

  1. Symbols are components. You just met four of them.
  2. Lines are connections — not actual positions. The drawing shows what connects to what, never where parts physically sit.
  3. Everything touching the same line is one point, electrically. That shared point is called a node.

The key move

A node is anywhere parts are squeezed together so firmly they become one electrical point. Today, a node is a finger pinch. Building from a schematic comes down to one job: give every node in the drawing its own pinch, then put the right legs into it.

5 The build: light it up

Partner up: this build takes four hands. One of you reads the schematic aloud and calls the nodes; both of you pinch. The rule of the pinch: your skin can't do the conducting — the metal parts must press directly against each other. Your fingers just provide the squeeze that holds them together.

  1. A  Pinch the metal tip of the supply's red (+) wire against either leg of the resistor — metal on metal, squeezed tight.
  2. B  Second pinch, other hand: the resistor's other leg against the LED's long leg.
  3. C  Your partner takes the moment of truth: the LED's short leg against the metal tip of the supply's black (−) wire. The instant the metals touch… light!
  4. Hold it lit and notice: you are holding a live circuit in your bare hands and feeling nothing. Five volts is far too low to push a meaningful current through skin.
  5. The free hand's job: trace the loop on the schematic — out of +, through A, the resistor, B, the LED, C, back into − — saying what happens at each stop.

No light? Run the Debugging Loop

Dead circuits are normal — engineers spend most of their time exactly here. Your kit includes the Debugging Loop card: six steps, in order, starting with stop, look, touch nothing. When something's dead: run the loop, check your build against the schematic, ask a neighbor — then wave a teacher over. And when you do, report what you found ("power's good, the loop traces, but I'm not sure about node B"), not just "it doesn't work."

The math, for the curious — always optional

Everything you just built has numbers behind it: the supply provides 5 V, the resistor resists with 220 Ω, and a rule called Ohm's law predicts the flow:

current=voltageresistance

— about 0.014 A (14 milliamps) through your LED. You will never be required to compute anything in this class. But the math is real, it lives in these purple boxes every lesson, and it's how engineers size every part before touching one.

6 What just happened?

You made light. Now the why — and it fits in one picture. Think of a closed loop of water pipes. A pump pushes the water, a narrow spot limits how fast it can flow, and a waterwheel uses the flow to do something. The water goes around and around — it never gets "used up," and if you cut the pipe anywhere, everything stops at once. Keep that in mind — you'll test the "anywhere" part in Experiment 2.

Three words you'll use all year:

  • Voltage — the electrical pressure that drives current around the loop; think of it as how hard the source pushes. Measured in volts (V). Ours supplies 5 V.
  • Current — how much electricity is actually flowing. Measured in amps (A).
  • Circuit — the complete loop the current flows around.

7 Experiments — predict first!

Rule for the whole year: say your prediction out loud before you try it. Being wrong is where the learning is.

Experiment 1 — Flip the LED

Predict

If you turn the LED around (swap which leg connects where), what will happen?

Try it

Re-pinch the LED the other way around — swap which leg is in which pinch. What happened, and what does that tell you about LEDs? This one-way behavior has a name: the LED is a diode.

Experiment 2 — Break the loop

Predict

If you break the loop at node C — after the LED — does the LED stay lit, since the electricity already "got there"?

Try it

Open the loop at A, then B, then C. Anywhere you break it, everything stops instantly. Current doesn't fill up a circuit like water in a cup — no loop, no flow, anywhere.

8 Words to know

circuit
a complete loop that current can flow around
node
a connection point — everything joined there is one electrical point (today: a finger pinch)
voltage
the power source's push, in volts (V)
current
the flow of electricity, in amps (A)
conductor
a material current flows through easily
insulator
a material that blocks current
LED
light-emitting diode — makes light, one direction only
resistor
limits current; protects the LED
schematic
the symbol drawing that is a circuit's recipe
short circuit
a loop with nothing to limit the current — the one thing we don't build

9 Exit ticket

On a scrap of paper, draw today's schematic from memory — power source, resistor, LED, in a loop, symbols only. Label + and −. Hand it in on your way out. (Wobbly lines are fine; correct connections are the point.)
