The Alarm

The big idea

A whole gadget is stages handing signals to each other. The 555 has a pin made for that: pin 4, its run/stop input — held high, the chip runs; pulled low, it stops, whatever its timing parts are doing. Wire a light-controlled transistor switch to pin 4 and the light in the room decides when the tone plays: the LDR and divider are the input, the transistor makes the decision — the processing — and the 555 and speaker are the output. That's an alarm. And pin 4, which cares only whether it's high or low, is your first digital input.

The basics

3 Warm-up: the night-light, from memory

Kits closed, one minute: draw lesson 9's night-light from memory — the sensing stage (100 kΩ over the LDR), the tap, the base resistor, the transistor switch with its LED. Blanks and guesses are fine. Keep the drawing out: you'll build from it in a few minutes. Then open the box and power on lesson 11's tone generator: you should hear its note. (Silent? 4 Pin 4: run and stop

Since lesson 10, pin 4 has had one jumper from it to the + rail, and the pin map called it the stop button. That jumper is a control wire — lesson 7's trick, aimed at a chip. Its free end can sit in one rail or the other, never both.

Pin 4's wirePredictHeard / saw
In the + rail — as it is now— (your note)
Moved to the ground rail

Power on, then pull the wire's free end out of the + rail and plug it into the ground rail. Silence — and pin 3's LED goes dark. Pin 4 is the 555's run/stop input: high (near +5 V) means run, low (near 0 V) means stop, regardless of what the timing parts are doing. It doesn't care about anything in between — just high or low. A signal that matters only as high or low is called digital, and next semester is built from them.

Leave pin 4's wire in the ground rail while you build the next part: the chip stays stopped.

5 The light switch

Pin 4 wants a high or a low. Lesson 9's night-light makes exactly that at its collector: low when its transistor is on (dark), high when it's off (light). One change, though. In lesson 9 the transistor switched an LED, which needs about 14 mA. The divider only feeds the base a small current, and a transistor can carry about 100 times its base current at most — not enough for an LED's 14 mA. Pin 4 needs almost nothing, so here the transistor switches a single 10 kΩ, a load it can pull all the way down.

  1. Power off. On free rows beside the chip: 100 kΩ (brown · black · yellow) from the + rail to an empty row — the tap. If your teacher says "two," use both 100 kΩ end to end, as in lesson 9.
  2. LDR from the tap row to the ground rail, face up.
  3. Transistor into three free rows — flat face toward you, E · B · C, said out loud. Emitter row → ground rail.
  4. 4.7 kΩ from the tap row to the base row. Trace it: the base's only way in is through the 4.7 kΩ.
  5. 10 kΩ (brown · black · orange) from the + rail to the collector row.

Black probe in the ground rail, red probe on a stub in the collector row. Power on, predict, measure:

Light on the LDRPredict: collector high or low?Measured
Room light
Cupped under both hands

Room light: the transistor is off and the 10 kΩ holds the collector near 5 V — high. Cupped: the tap climbs, the transistor turns on, and the collector drops to nearly 0 V — low. A light-controlled high-or-low: exactly what pin 4 listens for.

6 One wire

Moved, not added

Pin 4 gets one wire, and it lands in one place. Take its end out of the rail before it goes into the collector row — never a wire to a rail and a wire to the collector at the same time.

  1. Power off. Pull pin 4's wire out of the ground rail.
  2. Plug that same free end into the collector row. Trace it on the drawing: collector → pin 4.

Predict

In room light, the collector is high. With the LDR cupped, it's low. What will the speaker do in each — and why? Commit first:

Power on. Room light: the alarm sounds. Cup your hands around the LDR — or close it inside your kit box — and it goes quiet; open up and it sounds again. That's a box alarm: keep something dark, and the moment light gets in, the 555 is told to run. Follow the signal: light on the LDR → the tap sits low → the transistor is off → the 10 kΩ holds the collector high → pin 4 is high → the chip runs → the speaker sounds. Darkness flips every link in that chain. Three stages, three jobs — sensing, deciding, sounding — joined by wires that each carry one voltage.

(Not working? Power off and run the Debugging Loop, then test each block alone: pin 4's wire back on the + rail should make the tone, and the collector should read high in light and low when cupped. The Sensor Voltage Check card's check 2, the sensing stage alone, works here unchanged.)

Part 1 is done when…

  • Pin 4 by hand: in the + rail the tone plays; in the ground rail it stops.
  • The collector reads high in room light and low when you cup the LDR.
  • Your alarm sounds in the light and goes quiet in the dark — and you can say what the transistor does to pin 4 when it's dark.

All three? That's the lesson — and the semester in one gadget: a divider, an LDR, a transistor, a capacitor, a chip and a speaker, every one of them a part you met on its own first. The words and the exit ticket are right below; Part 2 comes after them, if you want more.

7 Words to know

run/stop input
pin 4 on the 555 (its datasheet name is RESET): high, the chip runs; low, it stops
high · low
near the + rail · near ground — the only two answers a digital input listens for
digital
a signal that matters only as high or low, never in between
load
whatever a switch is switching; the transistor can only fully switch a load it can carry — an LED's 14 mA is too much here, a 10 kΩ isn't

9 Challenge: the tripwire

Spec, no recipe: a flashlight beam shines on the LDR; the alarm stays quiet while the beam is there and sounds the moment something breaks it. That's the box alarm turned inside out — it has to sound in the dark instead of the light. One part of the build changes: the divider. Two decisions are yours — which slot the LDR goes in, and how big the fixed resistor must be. Lesson 9's beam detector (if you built it) holds both answers; if you didn't, measure the LDR in the beam and in room light first, and use the bigger-share rule. Draw it, then build it, then test it with a hand through the beam.

Drawing only

No numbered steps — your drawing is the whole recipe. Pin 4 stays on the collector; nothing right of the transistor changes. Read, wire, verify.

10 The two-tone siren

Real sirens don't hold one note; they swap between two. Your kit has a spare 555. Build lesson 10's slow blinker with it — 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ, 100 µF, its own pins 8 and 4 on the + rail and pin 1 on ground — on free rows of the board. Then one connection between the chips:

Predict first: when the slow chip's output goes high, does the note go up or down? Then listen. Pin 5, the control pin, has sat unused since lesson 10: it sets where the ⅓ and ⅔ marks sit. Pull it lower and the marks move closer together, the capacitor has less distance to travel, and the note rises. Now the alarm doesn't just sound — it wails.

11 More to try

For the fast and the curious

1. Why the LED had to go: put lesson 9's LED and 220 Ω back in place of the 10 kΩ on the collector. Predict: will the alarm still go quiet in the dark? Measure the collector, cupped. (The math box has the reason.) 2. The entry delay: real alarms give you a few seconds to get in. Put the 1000 µF from the tap to the ground rail — stripe to ground, out loud. Predict what happens when you open the box now. 3. Sensitivity: if you're running one 100 kΩ, add the second in series — does the alarm now trip in dimmer light, or need brighter? Predict, then test in a corner of the room. 4. The memory problem: close the box again and your alarm goes quiet — a burglar just shuts the lid. A real alarm keeps sounding once it's tripped. Which lesson-10 build held its last answer after you let go? You don't have a spare part for it today, but you've already met the idea — next semester builds it from logic gates.

The math, for the curious — always optional

Why the LED couldn't stay on the collector. Cupped, the divider behaves like about 2.5 V behind about 50 kΩ, which lets only about 35 µA into the base. A transistor with a gain of 100 can carry at most:

100×0.035 mA=3.5 mA

The LED's loop wants about 14 mA, so the transistor can't fully close — the collector stalls around 2 V, which pin 4 still reads as high. The 10 kΩ asks for far less:

5 V10000 Ω=0.5 mA

— well inside 3.5 mA, so the transistor pulls the collector all the way down. Pin 4 stops the chip below about 0.4 V and lets it run above about 1 V; the collector swings from about 0.1 V to about 4–5 V, far past both marks. That gap is a noise margin, and every digital chip next semester is designed around one.
