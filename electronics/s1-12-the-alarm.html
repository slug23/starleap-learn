The Alarm

The big idea

The output of one circuit can be the input of another. Today a light-sensing switch controls the 555's pin 4, so the alarm stays silent in the light and sounds when the light is blocked.

Part 1

The basics

No new parts — the semester's parts in one gadget. Lesson 11's tone generator is standing in your box; you'll add a light switch beside it and join the two with one wire. This part is the lesson: finish it and you've done the day's work. Part 2, further down, is extra.

1 Today you will…

  • Find the 555's run/stop pin and work it by hand.
  • Build a shadow switch — lesson 9's light switch with its divider flipped — and tune it to your room with the meter, in silence.
  • Join the two with one wire: a shadow alarm, quiet in the light, sounding the moment the light is blocked. Then the room lights go out.

2 Your kit today

  • Lesson 11's tone generator, standing in your box — 555, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ, 100 nF, LED and speaker each with a 220 Ω — plus jumpers and the multimeter
  • For the shadow switch: lesson 9's LDR, the pot, a transistor, a 4.7 kΩ, and a second 10 kΩ
  • For Part 2, if you get there: the spare 555, another 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ, the 100 µF and 1000 µF, both 100 kΩ, a 470 Ω, a flashlight

3 Warm-up: the night-light, from memory

Kits closed, one minute: draw lesson 9's night-light from memory — the sensing stage (100 kΩ over the LDR), the tap, the base resistor, the transistor switch with its LED. Blanks and guesses are fine. Then mark on it: is the transistor on in the light, or in the dark? Today's build flips that. Then open the box and power on lesson 11's tone generator: you should hear its note. (Silent? Check pin 4 is on the + rail and the speaker still has its 220 Ω — then the Debugging Loop.) Power off.

4 Pin 4: run and stop

Since lesson 10, pin 4 has had one jumper from it to the + rail, and the pin map called it the stop button. That jumper is a control wire — lesson 7's method, used on a chip. Its free end can sit in one rail or the other, never both.

Power on, then pull the wire's free end out of the + rail and plug it into the ground rail. Silence — and pin 3's LED goes dark. Pin 4 is the 555's run/stop input: high (near +5 V) means run, low (near 0 V) means stop, regardless of what the timing parts are doing. It reads any voltage as one or the other; between about 0.4 V and 1 V the answer isn't reliable, so circuits keep their signals well clear of that band. A signal read only as high or low is called digital, and next semester is built from them.

Leave pin 4's wire in the ground rail while you build the next part: the chip stays stopped, and the room stays quiet.

5 The shadow switch

Pin 4 wants a high or a low, and a transistor switch makes one at its collector: low when the transistor is on, high when it's off. Lesson 9's night-light turned its transistor on in the dark. An alarm that stays quiet in the light needs the opposite — transistor on in the light (pin 4 low, quiet) and off in the dark (pin 4 high, sounding). So flip the divider: the LDR goes on top, and under it the pot, wired as an adjustable resistor. By the bigger-share rule, in light the LDR is small, the bottom takes the bigger share, and the tap sits high; block the light and the LDR outgrows the bottom, and the tap falls. The pot is your sensitivity knob: it sets how dark counts as dark. One more change from lesson 9: the transistor switches a 10 kΩ instead of an LED — pin 4 needs almost no current.

  1. Power off. On free rows beside the chip: LDR from the + rail to an empty row — the tap. Face up.
  2. The pot as an adjustable resistor, lesson 5's way: wiper (middle leg) into the tap row, one outer leg into the ground rail, the other outer leg in an empty row of its own.
  3. Transistor into three free rows — flat face toward you, E · B · C, said out loud. Emitter row → ground rail.
  4. 4.7 kΩ from the tap row to the base row. Trace it: the base's only way in is through the 4.7 kΩ.
  5. 10 kΩ (brown · black · orange) from the + rail to the collector row.

Tune it — in silence

Pin 4's wire is still in the ground rail, so the chip can't make a sound. Black probe in the ground rail, red probe on a stub in the collector row. Power on.

  1. Turn the pot all the way one direction, then the other. At one end the collector reads high (near 5 V), at the other low. Leave it at the high end.
  2. Now turn it slowly toward the other end, watching the meter. Stop the moment the collector drops under 0.2 V — then turn a little further, so a person walking past doesn't set it off.
  3. Cover the LDR with your hand. The collector should jump back to high. Uncover it: low again.

Room light: the tap is high enough to turn the transistor on, so the collector is low. Covered: the tap falls, the transistor turns off, and the 10 kΩ pulls the collector high. The knob set the dividing line between the two — for your table, your lamp, your window.

Collector stays low at both ends of the pot? Move the red probe to the tap and turn the pot again. Don't go by the base: while the transistor is on, its base reads about 0.7 V no matter how much current flows in, so it barely moves. The tap is the reading that matters — it has to fall below about 0.6 V to turn the transistor off. If it never gets that low, check the divider: the LDR's leg in the + rail and the pot's outer leg in the ground rail (swapped, the divider is upside down), the pot's wiper — the middle leg — in the tap row, and only one outer leg used.

6 One wire

Moved, not added

Pin 4 gets one wire, and it lands in one place. Take its end out of the rail before it goes into the collector row — never a wire to a rail and a wire to the collector at the same time.

  1. Power off. Pull pin 4's wire out of the ground rail.
  2. Plug that same free end into the collector row. Trace it on the drawing: collector → pin 4.

Predict

In room light the collector is low; covered, it's high. Power on: what will the speaker do now — and when you cover the LDR? Commit first:

Power on. Silence. Cover the LDR with your hand and the alarm sounds; take your hand away and it stops. Follow the signal: light on the LDR → the LDR is small, so the tap is high → the transistor is on → the collector is low → pin 4 is low → the chip is stopped. Block the light and every link flips: the LDR grows, the tap falls, the transistor turns off, the 10 kΩ pulls the collector high, pin 4 goes high, the chip runs, the speaker sounds. Three stages — lesson 9's input, processing and output — joined by wires that each carry one voltage.

When yours works, leave it powered, hands off, and wait. The room lights have an appointment.

(Not working? Power off and run the Debugging Loop, then test each block alone: pin 4's wire back on the + rail should make the tone, and the collector should read low in room light and high when covered. Never goes quiet? Retune — the pot is too far toward the high end. Never sounds? The pot is too far the other way, or the tap isn't reaching the base.)

Part 1 is done when…

  • Pin 4 by hand: in the + rail the tone plays; in the ground rail it stops.
  • Tuned: the collector reads low in room light and high when you cover the LDR.
  • Your alarm is quiet in the light and sounds when the light is blocked — and you can say what the transistor does to pin 4 in the dark.

All three? That's the lesson — and the semester in one gadget: a divider, an LDR, a pot, a transistor, a capacitor, a chip and a speaker, every one of them a part you met on its own first. The words and the exit ticket are right below; Part 2 comes after them, if you want more.

7 Words to know

run/stop input
pin 4 on the 555 (its datasheet name is RESET): high, the chip runs; low, it stops
high · low
near the + rail · near ground — the two values a digital input reads
digital
a signal read only as high or low; voltages between the two aren't used
sensitivity
how much the input must change before a circuit switches — here, how dark counts as dark, set by the pot
load
whatever a switch is switching — here a 10 kΩ, because pin 4 needs almost no current

9 Challenge: the tripwire

Spec, no recipe: a flashlight beam shines on the LDR; with the room lights on, the alarm stays quiet only while the beam is there, and sounds the moment anything breaks it. Your shadow alarm already sounds in the dark — but ordinary room light keeps it quiet, and a tripwire has to treat room light as "broken." So the dividing line has to move: room light alone must count as dark, and only the beam as light. Decide which way the knob must go — or whether a fixed resistor from your assortment does the job better — using the bigger-share rule and your LDR's resistance in the beam and in room light (measure both first, power off, meter on Ω). Draw it, then build it, then test it with a hand through the beam. One guard: put a 470 Ω in series with the pot, so the knob turned to zero can never leave the LDR alone across the supply in a point-blank beam.

Drawing only

No numbered steps — your drawing is the whole recipe. Pin 4 stays on the collector; nothing right of the transistor changes. Read, wire, verify.

10 The two-tone siren

Real sirens don't hold one note; they swap between two. Your kit has a spare 555. Build lesson 10's slow blinker with it — 1 kΩ, 10 kΩ, 100 µF, its own pins 8 and 4 on the + rail and pin 1 on ground — on free rows of the board. Then one connection between the chips:

Predict first: when the slow chip's output goes high, does the note go up or down? Then listen. Pin 5, the control pin, has sat unused since lesson 10: it sets where the ⅓ and ⅔ marks sit. Pull it lower and the marks move closer together, the capacitor has less distance to travel, and the note rises. Now the alarm doesn't just sound — it wails.

The math, for the curious — always optional

Why the LED couldn't stay on the collector. Tuned just past the switch point, the base gets only a few tens of microamps — say 30 µA. A transistor with a gain of 100 can carry at most:

100×0.03 mA=3 mA

The LED's loop wants about 14 mA, so the transistor can't fully switch on — the collector stalls around 2 V, which pin 4 reads as high, and the alarm sounds in the light. The 10 kΩ asks for far less:

5 V10000 Ω=0.5 mA

— well inside 3 mA, so the transistor pulls the collector all the way down. Pin 4 stops the chip below about 0.4 V and lets it run above about 1 V; the collector swings from about 0.1 V to about 4–5 V, far past both marks. That gap is a noise margin, and every digital chip next semester is designed around one.
