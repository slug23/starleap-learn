Blinker Voltage Check

The idea

A build can look right and still be wrong. The meter doesn't look — it measures. Every row of a working blinker sits at a voltage you can know ahead of time. Walk the rows in order, compare each reading to this card, and stop at the first one that disagrees: the bug is touching that row.

Before you start

  • The Debugging Loop comes first. Power, trace, nodes, one-way parts. This card is for the build that passes the loop and still won't blink.
  • Meter on V⎓. Black probe on a wire stub in the ground rail, and it stays there — one reading on this card moves it, and says so. The red probe does the visiting: touch it to a component leg or a bare wire stub in the row.
  • Read the unit. 150 mV is 0.15 V — nearly zero, not one hundred fifty.
  • Write every reading in the Yours column, good or bad. Four checks, in order: each one assumes the checks before it passed.

2 Each switch, alone

Power off. Pull both capacitors and give each the reset — a bare 220 Ω across its legs for a slow count of three. Power on. With the capacitors out, the board is back to two separate switches, both told on: both LEDs lit, steady. Nothing moves, so take your time.

If a reading disagrees

You readLook at
B near 5 VNo current is entering the base. Is the transistor's B leg in this row? Is its E leg reaching ground? Flat face toward you, E · B · C? If all three are right, suspect the transistor itself.
B near 0 VNothing is feeding the base. The 4.7 kΩ: one leg in W, the other in B? Or something else in the base row is tying it to ground.
B is right, but C is above 0.3 V and the LED is dim or darkThe base is asking and the transistor isn't delivering. Transistor in backwards — C and E trading places — or a base resistor that isn't 4.7 kΩ. Read its stripes: yellow · violet · red.
C near 0 V, LED darkThe switch is on, but nothing is arriving to switch. The break is above it: LED backwards, or a leg a row off. M tells you which side of the middle row the break is on.

Both switches match? Both are cleared. Whatever is wrong involves a capacitor.

3 Freeze one side

Power off. Capacitors back in, and say the line for each: "plus to collector, stripe to base." Power on. Now plug a jumper into the ground rail, push its free end into B1's row, and leave it there. You've ordered switch 1 off, and the blinker can't trade: LED 1 fades out, LED 2 stays lit. Wait five seconds, then measure.

Ground only

The freeze jumper goes from a base row to the ground rail. A base wired straight to the + rail is lesson 7's cooked transistor.

Then move the jumper's free end to B2 and measure the mirror image.

If a reading disagrees

You seeLook at
The held switch's LED keeps a glow, or its 220 Ω shows more than a few mVCurrent is still running through that LED, and the only place left for it to go is into the capacitor on that collector. A capacitor passes steady current when it's in backwards or damaged. Where is its stripe?
The held switch's C stays well under 3 VSomething is holding that collector row down. What else is plugged into it — a stripe leg? a neighbor's leg?
Both LEDs go darkThe two base rows are joined somewhere. Trace each capacitor: it starts on one switch's C and ends on the other switch's B.
Nothing changes when you plug the jumper inThe jumper isn't in the base row — or the transistor's B leg isn't.

Both sides match? Pull the jumper out. Within a second the LED you were holding dark takes its turn, and the trading starts.

Still stuck? Bring the numbers

A filled-in card is a bug report. "Checks 1 and 2 pass. Holding B1, switch 1's collector only reaches 1.2 V" gets you fast help — and usually you'll see the bug yourself while you're writing the sentence.
