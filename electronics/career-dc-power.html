Careers in DC Power

The idea

Say "electrician" and most people picture house wiring: outlets, breaker panels, AC. But a fast-growing part of the trade works in DC — the same kind of electricity as your kit — inside datacenters, battery plants, solar farms, and fast chargers. Same rules as your breadboard. Much bigger numbers.

The electrician most people never meet

The electricians you have seen at work wire homes, stores, and offices. That is skilled, essential work, and nearly all of it is AC: 120 and 240 V in a house, up to 480 V in a large building.

Follow the wires into certain buildings, though, and the AC ends. Anything that stores energy in a battery, makes it in a solar panel, or computes with it in a chip runs on DC. Somebody has to build those systems, test them, and keep them alive — and that somebody is usually an electrician or an electrical technician whose job most people have never heard of.

This report is about that work: where it is, what the jobs are called, how people get into them, and what you could do about it while you are still in high school.

Datacenters get most of this report, because that is where the most new work is right now.

Follow the power through a datacenter

A datacenter is a warehouse full of computers. From an electrician's point of view it is something else: a machine for delivering power that is never allowed to stop. Power arrives from the utility as AC at thousands of volts, and it has to reach chips that run on less than one volt of DC. Every step in between is a conversion, and every conversion is equipment that someone installs, tests, and maintains.

Three things to notice:

  • Batteries have always put DC in the building. A datacenter cannot blink when the utility fails, so it keeps rooms of batteries charged and waiting. They carry the entire load until the backup generators start and take over, usually in under a minute. The system that manages this is the UPS, the uninterruptible power supply — and a battery can only be DC. Every datacenter has had a serious DC system in it from the start.
  • The chip end is extreme. A single top-end AI processor can use roughly a thousand watts at less than one volt. That works out to more than 1,000 A flowing into a part smaller than your palm.
  • The middle is what's changing. The newest designs replace the AC that runs through the building with 800 V DC.

Why 800 volts

Computers live in cabinets called racks. A rack of ordinary servers uses perhaps 5 to 15 kW. The racks being built for AI use well over 100 kW each, and the chip maker NVIDIA has announced designs for 1 MW in a single rack — a million watts, roughly what 800 average American homes use, in one cabinet.

The rule that decides everything here is P = I × V (it has its own page on the math shelf: P = IV). The power is fixed by what the chips need, so the only choice left is the trade between volts and amps. Feed that rack at 54 V and the current is enormous — about fifteen times more than at 800 V. Enormous current needs enormous copper: NVIDIA's own figure is up to 200 kg of copper bar to feed one such rack at 54 V. Raise the voltage and the same power arrives on a fraction of the metal, with a fraction of the heat.

The math, for the curious — always optional

Power is current times voltage, so the current a load draws is

current=powervoltage

A 1 MW rack fed at 54 V:

1,000,000 W54 V≈18,500 A

The same rack fed at 800 V:

1,000,000 W800 V=1,250 A

About fifteen times less current. And the heat wasted in a wire grows with the square of the current, so the same piece of copper would waste about

15×15≈220 times less

For scale, the LED loop on your breadboard:

5 V×0.014 A=0.07 W

A 1 MW rack is about fourteen million of those.

Where this stands — fall 2026

NVIDIA announced its 800 V DC design in May 2025, for racks arriving around 2027. In August 2026, Google, Microsoft, and NVIDIA agreed to work toward shared 800 V DC standards through the Open Compute Project. As of this writing it is being built and tested, not yet running at scale — which means the people who will install and maintain it are being trained about now.

High-voltage DC is not breadboard DC

The 5 V on your bench is a safe teacher. Hundreds of volts of DC is a different animal, and in one way it is harder to handle than AC at the same voltage.

  • The arc that doesn't quit. AC voltage passes through zero 120 times every second, and each zero is a chance for a spark to go out by itself. DC never passes through zero. Open a switch that is carrying high-voltage DC and the current can keep jumping the gap as an arc — a channel of glowing gas that can be hotter than the surface of the sun — until something melts. Switches, breakers, and connectors have to be built and rated specifically for DC.
  • Batteries have no off switch. A battery string is live the day it is delivered and stays live. Solar panels are live whenever there is light on them.
  • Capacitors remember. You have watched a capacitor light an LED with the supply unplugged. Scale that to 800 V and equipment can stay dangerous after it has been switched off.

Concepts, not procedures

This is why the work is a licensed, trained trade and not a hobby. Professionals lock power sources off with their own padlock, prove a conductor is dead using a meter they have just proven is working, and wear gear rated for arcs. None of that is learned from a web page. It is learned in an apprenticeship, beside people who do it every day — which is exactly why it is a career.

Watch the titles

"Data center technician" usually means the computer-hardware job: installing servers, swapping drives, running network cable. It is good work, but a different path. The power jobs are the ones with facilities, critical environment, or engineering operations in the title.

And if you would rather design this equipment than install it, that road is an electrical engineering degree.

How people actually get in

There is no separate "DC electrician" license. An electrician's license covers AC and DC both, and most of an apprenticeship is AC, because most of the world's wiring is. People get into DC work by where they choose to work once they are in the trade. So the honest answer to "how do I become a datacenter electrician?" is: become an electrician, then steer.

The apprenticeship is the main door. It is a job, not a school you pay for: you earn a wage from the first day, working under licensed electricians, and take classes alongside.

  • Length: four to five years, with about 2,000 hours of paid on-the-job training each year plus classroom instruction.
  • Who runs them: union programs (the IBEW electricians' union together with the contractors' association NECA, through about 300 local training centers), and non-union programs run by contractor groups such as IEC and ABC.
  • What the union programs typically ask for: age 18, a high school diploma or GED, one full year of high school algebra, a driver's license, an aptitude test, and an interview. The aptitude test has two parts: algebra, and reading comprehension.
  • At the end: you are a journeyman. Most states then require passing a licensing exam.

Many programs get more applicants than they have openings. Algebra and reading are how they sort the pile.

Other doors:

  • Community college and trade school. Two-year programs in electrical technology lead to technician jobs directly. Some colleges near large datacenters run datacenter-specific programs with the companies themselves; Microsoft's Datacenter Academy is one example.
  • The military. Electrical specialties in any branch transfer well. Datacenter operators recruit veterans of the Navy's nuclear power program by name, because they arrive already trained to run a power plant by written procedure.
  • Company trainee programs. Some large operators hire trainees with no experience, or run short paid pre-apprenticeships through local colleges. These come and go, so check the companies' own career pages.

If you are homeschooled

Programs ask for a diploma or GED and a transcript that shows algebra. Ask your local training center how they want a homeschool transcript documented, and ask early — it is far easier to line up in tenth grade than in the week applications open.

What you can do now

  • Take algebra seriously. It is on the entrance test. Then keep going: bending conduit to fit a building is applied trigonometry, and so is AC theory.
  • Read hard things. Half the aptitude test is reading comprehension, and the National Electrical Code — the rulebook you would work from — runs to nearly a thousand pages.
  • Keep building. Get so comfortable with a meter and a schematic that troubleshooting by method is a reflex.
  • Find your local programs. Look up the apprenticeships in your area, when they take applications, and what they require. Many hold open houses.
  • Read three real job postings for "critical facilities technician." Write down what they require, and what shifts they list.
  • Talk to someone who does the work. Ask an electrician or a datacenter technician what an ordinary Tuesday looks like.
  • Protect the basics every program checks. A driver's license and a clean driving record. Drug testing is standard across the trade.

The honest trade-offs

  • It never closes. A datacenter runs every hour of every day. Operations jobs mean nights, weekends, holidays, and being on call.
  • You go where it is being built. Datacenters and solar farms go up where land and power are available, often far from cities. Construction electricians may travel or relocate for months at a time.
  • Procedure beats improvisation. One wrong breaker can take down a site that millions of people are using, so every step is written down, reviewed, and checked by a second person. Some people love that discipline. Some find it suffocating.
  • It is physical. Loud rooms, hot aisles and cold aisles, ladders and lifts, heavy cable — and on construction sites, all of it outdoors in whatever the weather is.
  • The hazard is real. Training and procedure manage it; they do not remove it.
  • It is a boom. The AI building spree is an enormous construction wave, and nobody can promise how long it lasts. The protection is the license: a journeyman electrician can move to hospitals, factories, transit, solar, or homes. Learn the trade, not just the trend.

The Bureau's outlook points to AI datacenters, solar and wind power, and upgrades to the power grid as reasons for that growth. Specialized work often pays above the median, and overtime and shift pay add to it — but pay varies a great deal by region and employer, so real postings in your own area are the best evidence. Apprentices start at a fraction of the journeyman rate, often near half, and step up as they advance.

One more sign of the demand: Google has funded the electrical industry's own training organization, with a stated goal of 100,000 more trained electrical workers and 30,000 new apprentices.

Words worth knowing

apprenticeship
paid training on real jobs under licensed workers, with classes alongside
journeyman
a fully trained electrician who has finished an apprenticeship
UPS
uninterruptible power supply: the batteries and converters that carry the load when the utility fails
rack
the cabinet that holds servers; the unit datacenter power is counted in
busbar, busway
solid bars of copper or aluminum that carry currents too large for wire
switchgear
the building-sized breakers and switches that route and protect power
rectifier / inverter
equipment that turns AC into DC / DC into AC
one-line diagram
the schematic of an entire building's power system
arc flash
the violent burst of heat and light when current jumps through air
lockout/tagout
locking a power source off with your own lock and tag before working on it
commissioning
testing a new facility's systems, failures included, before it goes live
critical facility
a building whose power is not allowed to stop
NEC
the National Electrical Code, the rulebook for electrical installation in the U.S.
